How it makes sound

Vibrating air

Sound is made when something vibrates. In a didjeridu, the air inside the tube vibrates.
  • The player’s lips vibrate against the end of the tube.
  • This makes the air inside the tube vibrate.
  • A sound wave comes out of the other end.

Types of instruments

Three ways to make a sound

Instruments are grouped by what vibrates.

Idiophone: the instrument itself vibrates when it is hit or shaken. Examples: clapsticks, seed-pod rattles such as the kulap.
Membranophone: a stretched sheet (a membrane) vibrates when it is hit. Example: drums such as the buljirr.
Aerophone: air vibrates inside or around the instrument. Examples: the didjeridu, leaf instruments.

Materials and design

The bore

The bore is the hollow space inside a tube-shaped instrument.
  • Termites hollow out some eucalypt trunks. This makes the bore.
  • A long, wide bore helps make deep, low-frequency sounds.
  • The material, length, width and shape all change the frequency and amplitude.
  • For a drum, a tighter membrane or a smaller drum gives a higher pitch.

Wavelength of a didjeridu note

λ = vf

v = speed of sound in air = 343 m/s. f = frequency in Hz.

Worked example. A didjeridu makes a note of 70 Hz.
λ = 34370 = 4.9 m

Knowledge over generations

Sound in culture

The knowledge of how to make and use instruments has been developed, tested and passed on over many generations.
  • Instruments support ceremony, storytelling and communication.
  • Low-frequency sounds have long wavelengths. They can carry a long way.
  • Instruments have been used to share messages and stories, as well as for music.
  • Some instruments are used only by certain people or at certain times.
