Chapter 9 summary: Energy transfer

One page of key ideas, equations and terms for lessons 9.1 to 9.11. Open a lesson’s section, read it, then cover it and try the recall question.

The big ideas

  • Energy can move from place to place without matter travelling with it: as heat through particles, and as waves.
  • Waves are described by amplitude, wavelength, frequency and speed, linked by v = f × λ.
  • Mechanical waves (such as sound) need a medium. Electromagnetic waves (such as light) do not.
  • The ear and the eye change wave energy into electrical signals for the brain.
  • Technologies such as radar, ultrasound, lenses and instruments all rely on wave science.

Key equations

v = f × λ

speed (m/s) = frequency (Hz) × wavelength (m). Rearrange: f = v ÷ λ and λ = v ÷ f.

depth = v × t ÷ 2

Used for echoes (ultrasound). Divide by 2 because the sound travels there and back.

speed in a material = 300 000 km/s ÷ refractive index

The speed of light in a vacuum is about 300 000 km/s.

9.1 Energy and matter
  • Conduction: vibrating particles pass energy to their neighbours. Mainly in solids. Metals conduct well.
  • Convection: warm fluid is less dense and rises, cool fluid sinks, forming a current. Only in liquids and gases.
  • Radiation: energy travels as electromagnetic waves. It needs no matter, so it crosses a vacuum.
  • High thermal conductivity = heat passes quickly. Insulators have low thermal conductivity.

9.2 Wave types
  • A wave transfers energy, not matter. The particles only vibrate about a fixed position.
  • Transverse: particles move at right angles to the wave direction. Longitudinal: particles move parallel to it, forming compressions and rarefactions.
  • Mechanical waves need a medium. Electromagnetic waves do not.

Sound is a mechanical wave and needs particles to vibrate. Light is an electromagnetic wave and needs no medium.
9.3 Features of waves
  • Amplitude: height from the rest line. Wavelength (λ): distance between matching points. Frequency: waves per second, in hertz (Hz).
  • With the same speed, a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength. Amplitude and wavelength are independent.
  • A tsunami slows as it reaches shallow water: its wavelength shortens and its height grows.

9.4 Sound energy
  • Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave of compressions and rarefactions.
  • Amplitude sets the volume (measured in dB). Frequency sets the pitch (measured in Hz).
  • Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases, because particles are closer together in solids.
soundvolumepitch
Louder (larger amplitude) and higher pitched (higher frequency, shorter wavelength).
9.5 The Doppler effect
  • The Doppler effect is a change in the frequency a wave seems to have when a source and an observer move relative to each other.
  • Source approaching: waves bunch up, shorter wavelength, higher frequency, higher pitch. Source moving away: longer wavelength, lower frequency, lower pitch.
  • Uses: radar speed guns, weather radar, blood-flow measurement and the redshift of galaxies.

No. The source’s frequency is constant. The observer detects a different frequency because of the relative motion.
9.6 How we hear
  • Path: pinna → ear canal → eardrum → ossicles → cochlea → auditory nerve → brain.
  • Energy changes: sound wave → vibration of eardrum and bones → fluid motion → electrical signals.
  • Typical human hearing is about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. Below is infrasound and above is ultrasound.
  • Loud sounds can damage the hair cells in the cochlea, which do not grow back.

They pass on the vibrations from the eardrum and make them stronger before they reach the inner ear.
9.7 First Nations peoples’ use of sound energy
  • Idiophone: the instrument itself vibrates. Membranophone: a stretched membrane vibrates. Aerophone: air vibrates.
  • Length, width, shape and material change the frequency and amplitude. A longer tube gives a lower frequency.
  • Instruments are part of culture. Names, designs and protocols differ across Nations, so use endorsed sources.

An aerophone, because the air inside it vibrates.
9.8 The electromagnetic spectrum
  • Order, longest to shortest wavelength: radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays.
  • As wavelength decreases, frequency and energy increase. All travel at about 300 000 km/s in a vacuum.
  • High-energy waves such as UV, X-rays and gamma rays can damage cells, so they need safety precautions.
electromagnetic spectrumphotonUV
Bones absorb more X-rays than soft tissue, so they show clearly on the image.
9.9 Light waves
  • Reflection: light bounces off a surface. Angle of incidence = angle of reflection, both measured from the normal.
  • Refraction: light bends when it passes into a different material because its speed changes. Entering a denser material it bends towards the normal.
  • Absorption and scattering are the other two behaviours. Materials are transparent, translucent or opaque.
  • Convex lenses bring rays together. Concave lenses spread them apart.

It slows down and bends towards the normal (refraction).
9.10 How we see
  • Path: cornea → pupil → lens → retina → optic nerve → brain. The cornea and lens refract light.
  • Near objects: lens muscle contracts and the lens gets thicker. Distant objects: the lens gets thinner.
  • Rods work in dim light (greys). Cones detect colour in bright light.
  • Short sight is corrected with a concave lens. Long sight is corrected with a convex lens.

It gets smaller so less light enters the eye.
9.11 Medical uses of sound
  • Ultrasound is sound above the range of human hearing. A probe sends pulses and detects the echoes from tissue boundaries.
  • Gel removes air gaps that would reflect the sound. Ultrasound has much less energy than X-rays.
  • A higher frequency gives a shorter wavelength and shows finer detail, but does not travel as deep.
  • Other uses: Doppler flow meters, therapeutic ultrasound and surgical aspirators.
