9.6 How we hear

Goal: explain how the ear works in relation to sound energy.
Textbook: Good Science 9, pp. 210–213.

The ear, step by step

The ear has three sections: outer, middle and inner. Tap each part.

Tap a part of the ear to see its job.

Energy changes form on the way through:

Sound wave (air)→Vibrating eardrum and bones→Moving fluid→Electrical signal
Pinna: the outer part of the ear that collects sound.
Membrane: a thin sheet of tissue, such as the eardrum.
Cochlea: the spiral in the inner ear that changes vibrations into nerve signals.
Audiogram: a graph showing the quietest sounds a person can hear at different frequencies.
Infrasound: sound below the range of human hearing, under about 20 Hz.
Ultrasound: sound above the range of human hearing, over about 20 000 Hz.

    Go further: protecting and restoring hearing

    Tap each card to read more.

    Hearing loss. Very loud sounds, or loud sounds over a long time, can damage the hair cells in the cochlea. They do not grow back. The louder the sound, the shorter the time it takes to cause damage. Earmuffs, earplugs and turning the volume down all help.
    Cochlear implants. If the hair cells are damaged, an implant can help. A microphone picks up sound, a processor turns it into electrical signals, and electrodes in the cochlea pass the signals to the auditory nerve.
    Animal hearing. Humans hear about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. Dogs can hear higher notes, bats use ultrasound far higher still, and elephants use infrasound to communicate over long distances.

    Public awareness poster

    Choose one loud sound: concert speakers, fireworks, headphones at full volume or construction machinery. Research it, then design a poster to help people prevent hearing loss. Include:

    • a labelled diagram of the ear showing how it processes sound
    • an explanation of how the ear detects sound, from vibration to nerve signal
    • how your chosen sound damages the ear, and which part is affected
    • the impact on hearing, such as trouble with certain frequencies or permanent damage
    • prevention tips: safe listening habits, ear protection and hearing check-ups

    Include the decibel level of your sound, from a reliable source.
