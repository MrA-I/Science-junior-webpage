9.7 First Nations peoples’ use of sound energy

Goal: outline how the materials Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples use to make traditional musical and communication instruments affect their sound energy.
Textbook: Good Science 9, pp. 214–217.
Success criteria
  • MustI can identify one application of traditional knowledge about sound.
  • ShouldI can explain how traditional practices show an understanding of energy transfer through different mediums.
  • CouldI can analyse how the design of a traditional instrument relates to frequency and amplitude to produce its sound.
Working respectfully

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are many different Nations, each with their own instruments, names, designs and protocols. Some instruments are used only by certain people or on certain occasions. This lesson gives examples from your textbook. For more, use sources endorsed by your local Traditional Owners, and ask your teacher if you are unsure.

Materials and design

The material, length, width and shape of an instrument all affect the frequency and amplitude of its sound. Termites hollow out some eucalypt trunks, and a long, wide bore (the hollow inside) helps produce deep, low-frequency sounds.

Instruments are also part of culture. They support ceremony, storytelling and communication, and the knowledge of how to make and use them is passed on through generations.

Idiophone: an instrument whose own material vibrates to make sound.
Membranophone: an instrument with a stretched membrane that vibrates.
Aerophone: an instrument that makes sound by vibrating air.
Bore: the hollow space inside a tube-shaped instrument.

Go further: traditional and modern sound technology

Tap each card to read more.

Materials. Different woods, tube lengths, drum sizes and membrane tightness all change how an instrument vibrates. That is the same science used when engineers choose materials for modern speakers and guitars.
Sound for communication. Low-frequency sounds have long wavelengths and can carry far. Instruments have been used to share messages and stories, as well as for music and ceremony.
Working respectfully. Use sources endorsed by local Traditional Owners. Names, materials and protocols differ across Nations, so avoid assuming that one example applies everywhere. Never copy or reproduce sacred designs without permission.

Traditional and modern instruments

Research one traditional Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander instrument (for example the didjeridu, clapsticks or kulap) and one modern sound device (for example a speaker, electric guitar or microphone). For each:

  • describe how it produces sound waves
  • identify the scientific ideas involved, such as vibration, frequency and amplitude
  • for the traditional instrument, explain its cultural significance, using endorsed sources

Then compare them: give at least two similarities and two differences in how sound is generated.

Support: use a comparison table with sentence starters. Extend: evaluate how cultural knowledge and modern engineering have each influenced contemporary sound technology.
